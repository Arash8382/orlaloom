"Cozy sunroom" searches on Pinterest are up more than 900% this year, and it is not hard to see why — a sunroom is the one room in most houses that is already doing half the design work for you before you buy a single thing. The light is free. The problem most people run into is furnishing one on an actual budget without it turning into a patio-furniture showroom, and without every fabric surface fading out within a year of full sun exposure.

That second part is the detail most decor guides skip. A sunroom is not just a living room with more windows — direct, sustained sun changes what materials hold up. Natural fibers, treated wood, and UV-rated rugs do better here than the velvet and dark wood that work in a north-facing living room. Everything below is picked with that in mind, not just for the look.

## How to choose

Start with the one piece you will actually use every day: the chair. A sunroom's whole job is giving you somewhere to sit in the light, so it is worth spending more on seating than on any other single item — the Wickerix rattan armchair here is the anchor, and everything else in the room should support it rather than compete with it.

From there, work outward in three layers. The window layer comes first, because sunrooms are usually walled in glass and unfiltered midday sun is harsh, not cozy — a semi-sheer linen-look curtain softens it without losing the light that makes the room worth having. The floor layer comes next: whatever rug you pick needs to be rated for sun exposure and easy to wipe down, since a sunroom floor sees more UV and more incidental spills (watering cans, muddy shoes from the yard) than any other room. The surface layer is last — a small side table for your coffee, a tiered plant stand if you want greenery, and a heavy throw for the hours before the sun is fully up.

One more practical note: if your sunroom gets full sun for more than a few hours a day, budget for fading. Natural rattan, jute-look rugs and cotton throws all hold their color better than dyed synthetic fabrics, but nothing is fade-proof under glass in direct sun for years on end — rotating cushions and throws seasonally, or adding the sheer curtain layer, is the realistic fix rather than chasing a "UV-proof" product that does not really exist at this price point. One thing worth adding before you buy more furniture: a mirror. [Vintage arched and scalloped mirrors](/blog/vintage-arched-scalloped-mirrors) bounce the light back into the room and make a narrow sunroom read twice as wide.

## FAQ

**Will rattan furniture actually hold up in a sunroom, or will it dry out?**
Natural, unlacquered rattan like the armchair above can dry out and become brittle after years of constant direct sun and heat, the same way it would on an uncovered porch. It is not a fast process, and a sheer curtain layer that softens the harshest midday hours meaningfully slows it down. If your sunroom gets sun most of the day year-round, a lacquered or synthetic-wicker piece will outlast natural rattan, at the cost of some of the handmade texture.

**Does the rug need to be different from a regular living room rug?**
Yes, in one important way: it should be rated for sun exposure and easy to clean, not just soft underfoot. Wool and many synthetic pile rugs fade visibly within a year or two under sustained direct sun, where indoor/outdoor woven styles like the jute-look pick above are made to handle it. If your sunroom only gets a few hours of indirect light, a regular indoor rug is fine.

**How many plants is too many for a small sunroom?**
There is no fixed number, but a tiered stand is the practical ceiling for a small space — it lets you group plants vertically instead of spreading pots across the floor, which is what actually starts to feel cluttered. If you are working with a genuinely small sunroom, one three-tier stand in a corner reads as intentional; scattered individual pots across the floor tend to read as overflow storage instead.
