A cluster of woven baskets is one of the warmest, most forgiving ways to fill a bare wall. Framed art needs measuring, matting and a budget; baskets are light, soft-edged, and instantly cottagecore — that handmade, gathered-over-time texture that a flat print just can't give you. They soften echoey rooms, add a natural material against painted walls, and look intentional even when you hang them a little crooked. Here are the styles worth buying and how to choose between a single statement piece and a full gallery cluster.

## What to look for before you buy

Woven wall baskets all photograph the same and behave very differently on an actual wall. Six things decide whether a set looks collected or looks like a kit.

**Depth versus flatness.** A flat "wall plate" sits almost against the plaster and reads as pattern; a deep bowl throws a shadow and reads as sculpture. A cluster of only flat pieces goes visually dead. Mix at least a couple of deep ones in.

**The weave scale.** Tight, fine weaves look refined and slightly formal; loose, chunky weaves look handmade and rustic. Fine weaves suit a bedroom or a hallway, chunky weaves suit a kitchen or a sunroom. Choose one and stay with it — mixing weave scales at random is what makes a wall look like clearance stock.

**Material and undertone.** Water hyacinth is grey-green and cool. Seagrass is greenish-tan. Jute is warm and slightly gingery. Rattan and wicker sit in the middle and yellow gently with age. Two undertones on one wall is fine; three starts to look muddled.

**Size range within the set.** A set where every piece is 12-14 in gives you no rhythm. Look for sets that span roughly 10 to 22 in, so you have anchors and fillers.

**How it hangs.** Some baskets ship with a sewn loop or a keyhole; many ship with nothing and expect you to hook the rim over a nail. The second kind sits a little proud of the wall and tilts. Check the listing photos for a hanger before assuming.

**Front door or interior wall.** Anything hung outside — even under cover — will bleach and go brittle. Buy the cheapest set you would be happy to replace for a porch, and save the hand-woven pieces for indoors.

## One big basket, or a whole cluster?

The first decision is scale. **A single statement basket** — like the scalloped BEBE BASK — works when you have a defined spot to fill: above a headboard, over a console, centered on a narrow wall. The scalloped edge is the detail that pushes it from generic boho into cottagecore territory, so it earns its place alone.

**A multi-basket cluster** is the better choice for a large, blank wall. Buying a matched set (the jute set of six, or the eleven-piece bundle) takes the guesswork out of mixing sizes, because the pieces are already designed to graduate from large to small. If you have a tall or awkward space — a stairwell, a high living-room wall — the **oversized water-hyacinth trio** fills vertical height fast without needing a dozen small pieces.

## Material changes the whole mood

Woven baskets aren't all the same look. **Wicker and rattan** read crisp and a little more polished — good for a tidier, grandmillennial cottage. **Jute and seagrass** are softer and more rustic, with visible fibers and warmer tones that suit a cozier, lived-in room. **Water hyacinth** sits in between: substantial and sculptural, often sold in a painted white or natural finish. If you want the baskets to recede and just add texture, stay tonal with your wall; if you want them to pop, a darker natural basket on a cream or sage wall does the trick.

## How to hang a basket gallery that looks collected

The trick to a cluster that looks styled rather than scattered: **lay it out on the floor first.** Arrange the baskets on the ground until the spacing feels balanced — largest piece slightly off-center, smaller ones filling the gaps — then transfer that layout to the wall. Keep gaps roughly even (a hand's width is a good rule) and let the cluster breathe rather than crowding it into a tight square.

For hanging, most flat woven baskets are light enough for a single nail or a small adhesive hook, but anything large or in a set should go on a proper picture hook or wall anchor — water-hyacinth and deep wicker have real weight. Check the back before you buy: some baskets have a built-in loop, others need a small sawtooth hanger or a dab of removable putty to sit flat. Mixing two or three materials in one cluster (a little rattan, some jute, one scalloped piece) almost always looks more charming than a perfectly matched set.

## Where woven wall baskets disappoint

**They shed.** Natural fibre sheds fine dust for the first few weeks, especially seagrass. If you hang a cluster over a sofa, expect to vacuum the cushions more often than you did before.

**Cheap sets warp.** Thin flat plates cup and go oval within a season in a room with swinging humidity — a bathroom, a sunroom, a kitchen above a stove. If the wall you want is a humid one, spend more on thicker weaves or accept a shorter life.

**Colour is not consistent between orders.** These are dyed and dried in batches. Buying a "matching" extra basket three months later usually gets you a noticeably different tone, which is the main reason to over-buy on the first order.

**They are not storage.** Almost every flat wall basket is decorative. The listings often photograph them holding towels; the hanging hardware is not rated for it. If you want wall storage that works, buy something built for it and anchor into a stud.

**They fight busy walls.** On a patterned wallpaper or beside heavily patterned [gingham cafe curtains](/blog/gingham-cafe-curtains), a basket cluster turns to noise. Baskets want a plain wall behind them — which is also why they pair so easily with the plain-wall approach in a [cottagecore bedroom](/blog/cottagecore-bedroom).

## Care and cleaning

Dust them with a dry paintbrush or the soft-brush vacuum attachment every month or so; a damp cloth pushes grit further into the weave. If a basket has genuinely got dirty, wipe with a barely-damp cloth and let it dry fully away from a radiator — fast drying is what cracks the fibre.

Keep them out of direct afternoon sun. Water hyacinth and seagrass bleach unevenly, so a basket on a south-facing wall will be two shades lighter on one side within a year. If the only wall you have is a bright one, rotate the cluster occasionally, the same way you would rotate anything else in a [sunroom](/blog/cozy-sunroom-decor).

If a rim frays, a dab of clear PVA on the loose end and a bulldog clip overnight stops it spreading. Do not trim it flush — cut fibre unravels faster.

Baskets also mix well with other flat wall objects. If you want more texture without more basket, alternate them with [decorative wall plates](/blog/decorative-wall-plates) or a couple of framed [vintage botanical prints](/blog/vintage-botanical-wall-art) — the change in material is what stops a big wall reading as one repeated idea.

## FAQ

**How many baskets do I need for a gallery wall?**
For an average wall above a sofa, five to seven pieces in graduated sizes usually fills it nicely. For a tall or wide feature wall, a nine- or eleven-piece set gives you enough range to build height without big gaps. When in doubt, buy one more than you think — it's easier to leave a basket off than to find a matching extra later.

**Can I put real plants or items in these wall baskets?**
Most flat woven "wall plates" are decorative only and shouldn't hold weight. Deeper wicker baskets can sometimes hold a faux stem or a lightweight dried bunch, but treat them as wall art first. For anything with real weight, use a basket designed for storage and anchor it properly.

**What wall color suits woven baskets best?**
Warm neutrals — cream, soft white, oatmeal, or a muted sage — let natural baskets glow without competing. On a dark or "moody cottage" wall, lighter baskets like the painted-white water-hyacinth trio stand out beautifully. Avoid hanging natural-tone baskets on a busy patterned wallpaper, where the texture gets lost.

**Do woven wall baskets work in a bathroom or kitchen?**
They can, but choose thicker, deeper weaves rather than thin flat plates, and keep them away from the direct steam path above a shower or a hob. Humidity swings are what make cheap flat baskets cup and go oval.

**How far apart should the baskets be hung?**
Two to three inches between edges is the sweet spot for a cluster that reads as one object. Wider than about five inches and the group falls apart into separate baskets; tighter than two and it looks like a stack.

**What do I hang them on?**
For anything under a pound, a hardwall picture hook or an adhesive strip rated well above the basket weight is enough. For the deep oversized pieces, use a proper anchor. Never hang a basket from a single nail through the weave itself — it tears.

**Are the sets worth it, or should I buy individually?**
Sets are better value and give you a coherent size range in one order, which is hard to achieve buying one at a time. Buy individually only when you want one specific statement piece, or when you already have a cluster and need a particular size.

**Will they yellow over time?**
Yes, gently, and it usually improves them — natural fibre warms as it ages. Uneven bleaching from direct sun is the version you do not want, which is why wall choice matters more than the basket does.
